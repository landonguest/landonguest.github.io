Traffic Flow Simulator

How traffic density turns free flow into gridlock

Every roadway has a breaking point. Add enough vehicles to a single lane and a smooth, free-flowing highway can flip into stop-and-go traffic, with no crash, no ramp, and no lane closure required. This simulator uses the Greenshields model, a foundational relationship in transportation engineering, to show exactly how that happens. Field note

In 2008, Japanese researchers placed real drivers on a circular test track and asked them simply to hold a steady, even speed. A traffic jam still formed on its own, rippling backward through the line of cars with no crash and no bottleneck involved. Density alone was enough. The looping road above is built on that same idea.

How the model works

Four steps turn one slider position into a full picture of roadway performance.

  1. Set a density. Choose how many vehicles occupy one mile of a single lane (k, in veh/mi/ln).
  2. The model computes speed. Greenshields' linear relationship, v = vf(1 − k / kj), predicts the average speed drivers can hold at that density.
  3. Speed and density combine into flow. Flow, q = k × v, is the number of vehicles passing a fixed point each hour, the figure engineers use to size roadways.
  4. Density sets the Level of Service. The same density is checked against Highway Capacity Manual thresholds to assign a letter grade from A (free flow) to F (breakdown).
Density (k)
Vehicles occupying one mile of a single lane, in veh/mi/ln.
Speed (v)
Average speed of vehicles in the traffic stream, in mph.
Flow (q)
Vehicles passing a fixed point per hour, in veh/hr/ln. Equal to k × v.
Free-flow speed (vf)
The speed drivers choose when there is essentially no other traffic.
Jam density (kj)
The density at which vehicles sit bumper to bumper and speed drops to zero.
Critical density (kc)
The density at which flow is maximized, equal to kj / 2 under this model.
Capacity
The maximum possible flow rate, equal to vf × kj / 4 under this model.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Greenshields' 1935 model assumes a straight-line relationship between speed and density. Real traffic data often fits other models, such as Greenberg's or Underwood's, more closely at very low or very high densities.
  • The model assumes one uniform lane with no ramps, weaving, curves, or incidents. Real freeways rarely behave this cleanly.
  • The Level of Service thresholds shown are simplified density cutoffs for a basic freeway segment. The full Highway Capacity Manual procedure also factors in free-flow speed category, heavy-vehicle adjustments, and terrain.
  • In practice, breakdown to LOS F often begins well before the mathematical capacity point shown here, since real driver behavior grows unstable before density theoretically maximizes flow. Push the slider past 45 veh/mi/ln to see that play out.
